## The Deep Dive: The Best Model in the World Scored 12%

On Monday, the research firm SemiAnalysis released GDPx, a benchmark of 206 long, realistic tasks across 11 professional domains, including chip design, biology, finance, legal work, quant trading and software engineering. The top performer, Claude Opus 5.5, scored 12.0%. Google's Gemini 4 Carbon came second at 10.9%, and OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra third at 9.6%.

I'll push back on the doom reading first. GDPx was built to be brutally hard on purpose, as a successor to OpenAI's GDPval, which SemiAnalysis considers too tidy and too easy to pass. Scores average three attempts per task, and the reporting is clear that the number is not the share of jobs AI can automate. A low score on a deliberately extreme test is not a verdict that AI can't help your team.

But it is a useful reality check, and the takeaway for a business is about how you delegate, not which model you pick.

Whole jobs fail; pieces succeed. "Run our quarterly close" is a GDPx-style task. "Reconcile these 40 invoices against the PO list and flag mismatches" is something AI does well today. The smaller and better defined the piece, the better the result.

Define "done" before you start. Hard tasks fail quietly when nobody said what a correct answer looks like. Write two or three sentences of acceptance criteria and give them to the AI up front.

Price the whole workflow, not just the tokens. In the reported results, Claude cost roughly $51 per task, Gemini just over $16 and GPT-6 Astra about $54. If the cheaper model gets you most of the way there and a human finishes the rest, that may beat paying more for the leader.

Keep a person on the last mile. Even the leader fails most of these tasks end to end. Anything that touches money, clients or legal exposure needs a human check before it goes out.

Notice what all four have in common: context and structure. The more clearly you tell AI what the job is, the less it has to guess.

## From the Field: Five Steps to Hand AI a Job It Can Actually Finish

If your AI results are hit or miss, the problem is usually the handoff, not the tool. Here's the routine I'd run before delegating anything meaningful.

1. Break the job into pieces. Write out the steps a new hire would follow. Hand AI one or two at a time, not the whole thing.
2. State what "done" looks like. Give the format, the length, what must be included and what to leave out. One short paragraph is enough.
3. Show an example. Paste one finished, good version of the output. A single real example beats a page of adjectives.
4. Make it check itself. Before it hands anything back, ask: "List what you were unsure about and anything you assumed." This surfaces the guesses you'd otherwise find too late.
5. Review the last mile yourself. You read everything before it goes out. AI does the legwork and you make the call.

Why this travels: every team has a task that's too big to trust to AI and too tedious to keep doing by hand. Slicing it into well-defined pieces is how you get something useful out of it.

### AI stocks take a $2.5 trillion beating, then bounce

### OpenAI announces GPT-6 Cyber

OpenAI announced GPT-6 Cyber, a model specialized in finding software vulnerabilities, simulating attacks and verifying fixes. Separately, OpenAI said an upcoming cyber model found, validated and patched 948 Linux privilege-escalation bugs in one pipeline run, at about $1,279 per flaw in API credits.

My take: Defenders get these tools, and so do attackers. If you run software or a website, the boring basics (updates, patching, access controls) matter more every month. [AI Weekly roundup](https://aiweekly.co/ai-news-today/edition/2026-11-05)

### UK cyber agency: AI-powered attacks on critical infrastructure likely by 2028

The UK's National Cyber Security Centre published its annual review on Tuesday. It expects nation states to be using AI in cyber operations by 2028 and AI-powered malware to be used in a ransomware attack on UK critical infrastructure. It also notes that many state-linked hacks exploit flaws where a patch existed but hadn't been applied in time.

My take: The most useful point in the report isn't about AI at all. It's about patching. Fixing old, known problems is still the cheapest defense you have. [NCSC report coverage](https://www.techuk.org/insights/news/ncsc-annual-review-the-state-of-the-cyber-nation)

### Meta buys AI coding startup Conductor

Meta acquired Conductor, whose software lets developers split a coding project across several AI agents working at once. Conductor's cloud service shuts down on November 20, after which customer data will begin to be deleted.

My take: Running multiple agents in parallel is becoming a standard way to build. If you use a small tool that just got acquired, export your data now, not on the deadline. [The Neuron digest](https://www.theneuron.ai/digest/everything-that-happened-in-ai-today-thursday-november-5-2026/)
