# Four Rides

Halloween week is one of the busiest weeks of the year for Uber. Roughly 320 million trips and orders went through the company in seven days.

Four of those rides were booked by an AI agent.

Not four percent. Four rides.

That number came out of Uber's earnings call on Wednesday, and it says more about where AI pays off than most of the keynotes this year. If you're trying to figure out where to point AI in your business (and where not to bother), this one is for you.

## The Deep Dive

### Four Rides

Here are the numbers. On its third-quarter call on November 4, Uber said that during the week of Halloween, four rides were ordered through an AI agent. Uber Eats did a little better: about 500 orders. Across both, the company handled around 320 million trips and orders that week. DoorDash said the week before that it had received only a few hundred orders from AI agents.

This isn't because nobody has an agent. Meta says its Muse assistant hit No. 1 in the U.S. App Store in October with more than 10 million users. Uber's CEO, Dara Khosrowshahi, said every AI company is talking to them.

So why four?

Khosrowshahi answered it himself. Booking a ride in the Uber app takes about 50 seconds. Reordering your usual takes about 90.

Read that again. There is almost nothing for an agent to save. You'd spend longer explaining to the agent where you're going than tapping the button.

The same week, Ramp published spending data from the businesses on its platform. AI token spending grew 1.5% across August and September, after growing 42% in July. The median company spends $12 to $15 per employee per month on AI. The top 1% spend close to $10,000 per employee per month.

Look at the gap between those two numbers. A small slice of companies has found work where AI saves real hours, and it is spending accordingly. Most companies are paying for a subscription or two and asking it the occasional question.

I'll push back on the pile-on a little first. "Agents are hype, only four rides" is the easy headline and it's wrong. Ramp's economist, Ara Kharazian, says the spending slowdown is mostly about price cuts and smarter buyers, not weaker demand. Token volume kept climbing, and spending bounced 8.9% between late September and late October. People are using AI more. They're just paying less per unit and getting pickier about where.

And four rides doesn't mean agents don't work. It means people are good at noticing when a tool doesn't save them anything. Nobody needs a robot to press a button they can press in 50 seconds.

Here is the part that matters. AI doesn't win by shaving seconds off things that are already fast. It wins on the work that takes an hour, involves five tabs and a spreadsheet, and gets pushed to Friday every week.

Now for the business part.

**Time the task before you automate it.** Before you buy or build anything, write down how long the job takes a person today. If it's under two minutes, leave it alone. Say you run a landscaping company. Scheduling a crew in your existing software is fast. Writing a custom quote from site photos, measurements and last year's pricing takes 45 minutes. One of those is a four-rides job. The other is where your money is.

**Look for the jobs people avoid.** The best targets usually aren't the most frequent tasks. They're the ones everyone quietly avoids: the quarterly client review prep, the month-end reconciliation, the proposal that sits half-written for a week. If someone sighs when it comes up, put it on the list.

**Don't measure success by your AI bill.** Spending more isn't the goal, and with prices falling your bill should flatten even as you use it more. The question is how many hours came back, and what your team did with them. (We covered that second question in October. It still applies.)

**Expect the median to move.** That $12 to $15 number tells me most teams haven't found their 45-minute jobs yet. That's not a criticism. It's an opening.

## From the Field

### Claude Can Read Your Bank Statements Now. Start With the Subscriptions.

On November 5, Anthropic launched Personal Finance in Claude. It's in beta for Pro, Max and Ultra subscribers in the U.S., rolling out this week.

You connect bank and credit card accounts through Plaid (Claude never sees your bank password), and Claude can see balances, credit limits and up to two years of transactions. Then you ask questions in plain English. It sorts spending into categories, compares months, and flags duplicate charges, unexpected fees and bills that have crept up over time. It can also build charts, a calendar of due dates, or a custom dashboard.

Back in October I wrote about a third-party finance connector for Claude. This is Anthropic's own version, built into the app.

It's designed for personal finances, so start with your own accounts. But if you're an owner who runs a few business expenses through a personal card (no judgment), you'll see why I like it. Here's the setup.

**1\. Connect one card.** Open Personal Finance from the Claude sidebar and connect the card where your recurring charges land. You approve read-only access in Plaid.

**2\. Ask for the creep.** Try: "List every recurring charge from the last 12 months. Show which ones went up, by how much, and when." This is where the duplicate seats and the forgotten trials show up.

**3\. Ask for the receipts.** For any number that surprises you, ask Claude which transactions support it. Anthropic's own guidance says Claude can make mistakes and to check key figures against your bank. A well-formatted answer is not a checked answer.

**4\. Decide, then act yourself.** It's read-only. It can't cancel, move or invest anything. You make the call and you make the change.

Two more details worth knowing: conversations inside the Personal Finance hub aren't used to train Anthropic's models, and you can unlink an account any time. Anthropic also says it isn't built to give investment advice. For my wealth management readers, note one line in the announcement: Anthropic says it's exploring ways for Claude to work alongside licensed financial professionals and is asking wealth managers for input. That's worth watching.

**Why this travels:** Every business has software subscriptions that grew a little each year without anyone deciding they should. A 20-minute review with Claude is a 45-minute job turned into a five-minute one, which is exactly the kind of work this issue is about.

### Three Stories From This Week

### Anthropic's IPO filing says self-building AI may be close

In a revised S-1 filed on November 2, Anthropic added risk factor language saying it uses AI in its own research and development, that it has in the past failed to notice significant speedups in its progress until after they happened, and that internal estimates suggest future models may soon meet its own definition of "dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI." The company says no current model has crossed that threshold, that it doesn't expect to safely let Claude build its successors without meaningful human supervision, and that it's considering updates to its Responsible Scaling Policy to keep humans in control. Skeptics point out that an S-1 is part disclosure, part marketing.

**My take:** Both things can be true. An IPO filing is a sales document, and it's also a legal document where overstating things gets you sued. I'd take it seriously. Notice the fix Anthropic chose: humans stay in the loop on the most important work. That's the same rule I'd give any business putting AI near money, customers or legal exposure. Nothing about your Tuesday changes, but your approval steps matter more every month. ### OpenAI gives defenders a cyber model, and a $1 billion pledge

On November 5, OpenAI announced ChatGPT Cyber, a security-focused model for the top tier of its Daybreak program, which is aimed at offensive security researchers hunting for vulnerabilities. OpenAI also announced a $1 billion initiative for people who protect critical infrastructure like the power grid and water systems, and says it wants to expand Daybreak to organizations of all sizes.

**My take:** This is good news, with an asterisk. The defenders get the best tools only after identity checks and program tiers. The attackers, as the next story shows, often just download something free. Most of my readers will never touch Daybreak, and that's fine. Your job is less exotic: make sure whoever handles your IT (in-house or outsourced) can tell you what they're doing differently now that finding holes is this cheap. [READ THE TECHCRUNCH REPORT →](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/05/openai-released-a-new-cybersecurity-model-is-that-enough/)

### Hundreds of AI hacking tools were sitting open on the internet

Security firm Censys found more than 800 deployments of Artex, an open-source autonomous penetration testing tool released in July, accessible on the web with no password. Microsoft has described Artex as a system of AI agents that can run an attack from reconnaissance through data theft with little human involvement. It has been linked to September attacks on South Korean financial firms and to the October ConnectWise breach, which hit thousands of organizations. Censys notes many instances belong to legitimate testers, and many of the linked attacks relied on weak or known passwords and no multi-factor authentication. TechCrunch reported the findings on November 6.

**My take:** Read that last part again. The scariest AI hacking tool of the year mostly got in through bad passwords and missing MFA. The attackers have AI now. Your defense is still boring. Two things worth doing this week: turn on multi-factor authentication everywhere it's offered, starting with email and banking, and ask your IT provider whether their remote access tools have it too.
