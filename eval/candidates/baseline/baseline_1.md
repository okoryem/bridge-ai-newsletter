# The Deep Dive

### Apple Put Its Engineers on an AI Diet. Don't Copy Them.

Here's what happened, all within a few weeks:

**Apple** quietly cut the daily AI spending cap for some engineers from **$300 a day to $200**, and is reportedly considering $100. Many non-engineering teams were already capped at $100 a day, and Apple has discussed dropping some of them to **$25**. (Bloomberg broke the story. It's part of new CEO John Ternus's wider cost push.)

**Accenture** cut its centrally funded Claude allowance to **$29 per employee per month**. Anything above that has to come out of a project or team budget.

**Goldman Sachs** limited Claude Code and GitHub's AI coding tools mostly to its engineers. That took them away from roughly **10,000 bankers and traders**, the same people Goldman's CIO praised in July for "vibe coding" their own tools.

On top of that, Menlo Ventures' new enterprise survey found the average large company planned to grow AI spending **1.9x** this year and actually spent **4.7x**. That's more than double the budget.

So the AI bill came in far over plan, and the CFOs reacted. Fair enough.

**The detail that matters most is buried in the Apple story: few engineers were actually using their full $300 a day.** That means the cap isn't fixing an overspending problem. It's fixing an *accountability* problem. Apple handed out a huge allowance without tying it to specific work, and now it can't tell which dollars produced anything.

That's the real lesson, and it applies just as much to a 15-person RIA as it does to Apple.

**1. Spend that isn't tied to a workflow is the problem, not spend itself.** If someone burns $200 a day building a tool that saves the firm 10 hours a week, that's the best money you'll spend all year. If someone spends $20 a month asking a chatbot to reword emails, that's a rounding error that produces nothing. Every AI dollar should map to a named workflow with a named owner.

**2. Cap by job, not by headcount.** Goldman's move gets mocked, but the instinct underneath it is right, even if the execution was clumsy. Not everyone needs a coding agent. Everyone needs a good assistant. Decide who in your firm *builds* (connects tools, writes skills, automates processes) and who *uses* what gets built. Give the builders a real budget. Give everyone else a standard seat.

**3. Measure hours returned, not tokens burned.** "We spent $4,000 on AI last month" tells you nothing. "We spent $4,000 and got back 120 advisor hours" tells you everything. Divide your AI bill by an advisor's loaded hourly rate and you'll see how low the bar is to clear.

Here's the opportunity for smaller firms. While the giants pull back, the per-seat price for most of us is still trivial. Accenture's new allowance is $29 a month. Apple's old cap worked out to about $6,000 a month per engineer. You don't need to choose between those numbers. You need to know what each dollar is buying.

# From the Field

### The 30-Minute AI Spend Audit

Whenever a big company makes AI-cost headlines, I get the same question from owners: "Are we wasting money on this?" Most can't answer it, and not because the numbers are hidden. Nobody has looked.

Here's the audit I'd run this week. It takes about half an hour.

**1. Pull the bill.** Open the admin console for every AI tool you pay for (Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, whatever's in your notetaker). Write down seats and monthly cost. Most firms find at least one subscription nobody remembers buying.

**2. Sort users into three buckets.** Daily users, occasional users, and people who haven't logged in for 30 days. That last bucket is free money. Reassign or cancel those seats.

**3. Find your top three users and ask one question.** "What do you use it for that you'd hate to lose?" Their answers are your firm's real AI workflows, and they're usually better than anything a vendor demoed for you.

**4. Write each workflow down.** One line apiece: who does it, how often, and roughly how long it took before. That's your hours-returned number.

**5. Pick one workflow to make official.** Turn it into a shared skill or template so the whole team gets it, not just the one person who figured it out.

You'll finish with a short list that tells you what you're paying, what you're getting, and where to put the next dollar. That puts you ahead of most of the Fortune 500 right now.

### Three Stories From This Week

### Anthropic's IPO Filing Says It May Be Close to AI That Speeds Up Its Own Development

Anthropic's revised S-1, filed November 2, includes an unusual risk factor. The company says its internal estimates for future models suggest it "may imminently meet" the threshold in its Responsible Scaling Policy for dramatically accelerated progress driven by AI itself. In plain English, Claude is getting good enough at helping build the next Claude that progress could speed up sharply. Anthropic says no current model has crossed that line. It also admits it has sometimes not noticed big capability jumps until after they happened, and says it's considering policy changes to "keep humans in control."

**My take:** Remember that an S-1 is part legal disclosure, part sales pitch. But for planning purposes, the practical takeaway is that the tools you're using today are the worst they'll ever be, and the gap between versions may be shrinking. Build processes around what AI does for your firm, not around one specific model's quirks.

### Four People Booked an Uber With an AI Agent Last Month

That's not a typo. During its busiest week in October (Halloween week), Uber says **four** rides were booked through AI agents. Uber Eats saw about 500 agent orders, against roughly 320 million trips and orders overall. DoorDash reports "several hundred" in total. Dara Khosrowshahi called the traction "very, very early and very limited." Part of the reason is that booking a ride in the Uber app already takes about 50 seconds.

**My take:** This is the most useful reality check of the year. Agents don't win where the existing process is already fast and pleasant. They win where the process is slow, repetitive and annoying: account opening paperwork, meeting prep, CRM cleanup, compliance review. Point AI at the 45-minute tasks, not the 50-second ones.

### Anthropic Now Has 55% of Enterprise AI API Spend

Menlo Ventures' 2026 State of Enterprise AI report, a survey of nearly 400 U.S. enterprise decision-makers, puts Anthropic at **55%** of enterprise LLM API spend, up from 40% a year ago. OpenAI rose from 27% to 31%. Google fell from 21% to 9%. Together, Anthropic and OpenAI now take 86% of the market. Open-source models handle about 19% of usage but only about 4% of spend. Menlo also estimates enterprise AI deployment spending grew from about $33B to $177B in a year.

**My take:** Disclosure: Menlo is an Anthropic investor, so take the framing with a grain of salt. Still, it matches what I saw on the Schwab IMPACT floor last week, where most of the "proprietary AI" I saw ran on Claude underneath. If your vendor's secret sauce is a model you could license directly, factor that into the price you're willing to pay.
