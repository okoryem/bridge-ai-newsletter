## The Deep Dive: Apple Just Put Its Engineers on an AI Allowance

Five weeks ago I wrote that [Anthropic loses money on its best customers](https://deanmazlish52.substack.com/p/anthropic-loses-money-on-its-best) and that you should be one of them. I stand by it. But this week the other side of the buffet showed up, and it's worth a look.

According to [Bloomberg, via TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/04/apple-is-reportedly-cutting-budgets-for-its-internal-ai-usage/), Apple cut its engineering teams' daily AI spending cap from $300 to $200 in October. More cuts are planned, with some allowances dropping to $100 a day. For people outside engineering, Apple is reportedly considering a limit as low as $25 a day.

Earlier this year the trend had a name: "tokenmaxxing." Companies told everyone to use AI as much as possible, as often as possible, on everything. Then the invoices arrived.

Apple isn't alone in second-guessing it. The same week:

- **The Bank of England** published its November Monetary Policy Report and [found "only evidence of a modest AI-driven rise"](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/11/06/bank-of-england-pours-cold-water-on-ai-productivity-boom/5308059) in UK productivity: about 0.5% total over three years. Most of the AI-related GDP growth came from *building* AI, not from other businesses *using* it.
- **A Gartner survey** cited in the same coverage found that only **3% of organizations had hit their AI targets**, 38% were already over budget, and 85% planned to spend even more next year.
- **AI stocks had a terrible Thursday.** After the midterms, [Nvidia fell 9.3%, AMD 17.3%, and Micron 17.5%](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/06/ai-data-center-midterms-selloff.html) on worries about data center restrictions and whether all this spending will pay off.

So is the party over? No. But the free-refills phase is.

**Here's the part I want you to notice:** almost none of these headlines say AI doesn't work. They say companies can't *show* that it works. "Over budget" and "missed targets" are measurement problems before they're technology problems. If you rolled out a tool to 200 people with the instruction "use it more" and no definition of success, you'd miss your targets too. You never set any.

I see the same pattern in wealth management and in the mid-sized businesses we work with. Firms land in one of three places:

1. **The Seat Buyers.** They bought licenses for everyone, ran one lunch-and-learn, and now see a monthly charge and a vague sense that "people like it." These are the firms that end up capping usage at $25 a day, because they can't tell the useful $25 from the wasted $25.
2. **The Tourists.** A few enthusiasts use AI brilliantly. Everyone else tried it once, got a mediocre answer, and went back to their old ways. The value is real but stuck in a few people's heads.
3. **The Receipt Keepers.** They picked three to five specific workflows, such as meeting prep, post-meeting CRM updates, client review packets, and compliance pre-checks on outgoing email. They measured how long each took before AI and how long it takes now. They know what each workflow costs and what it saves.

The Receipt Keepers never have to ration, because every dollar has a job. When Apple's finance team asks "what are we getting for $300 a day?", the honest answer is apparently "we're not sure." When your COO asks the same question, you want to answer with a number.

**My position hasn't changed:** use AI heavily. Heavy use of the right workflows is still the best deal in business software. Spending $200 a month so an advisor gets back six hours a week is not a cost problem. It's the cheapest hire you'll ever make. But "heavy" and "aimless" are different things. The firms that win the next two years won't be the ones spending the most or the least. They'll be the ones who can tell you, workflow by workflow, what they're getting.

And if your board or partners start reading these headlines and asking whether AI is a bubble, don't argue about Nvidia's stock price. Show them your scorecard. Which brings me to...

## From the Field: A One-Page AI Scorecard You Can Build This Week

You don't need a dashboard, a consultant, or a data team to start measuring. You need one page. Here's how we set these up with clients.

**Step 1: List the workflows, not the tools.** Write down the five tasks your team uses AI for most. Use verbs: "Draft meeting follow-up emails," "Prepare quarterly review packets," "Summarize new account paperwork." If you can't name five, that tells you something too.

**Step 2: Get a "before" time for each.** Ask the two or three people who do the task most often how long it took before AI. A rough answer is fine. "About 40 minutes per client meeting" is enough to work with.

**Step 3: Get an "after" time, including review.** This is where most firms fool themselves. If AI drafts the review packet in two minutes but someone spends 25 minutes fixing it, the after time is 27 minutes, not two. Always count the human review. You should always have one anyway.

**Step 4: Multiply by volume.** Time saved per task × times per month = hours back. Put a dollar value on those hours using a loaded hourly rate. Then put your monthly AI spend next to it.

**Step 5: Add one quality line.** Speed isn't everything. For each workflow, note whether quality went up, held steady, or went down. Follow-up emails that go out the same day instead of three days later are a quality win, even if the minutes saved are small.

**Step 6: Review it monthly, and cut ruthlessly.** Any workflow where the after time isn't clearly better than the before time should get fixed (usually with better context or a reusable skill) or dropped. Any workflow that's clearly winning should be rolled out to more people.

That's it. Six columns on one page. The first version takes about an hour. You'll probably find one or two workflows saving far more than you expected, and one that's quietly a waste of everyone's time.

## AI News Worth Your Time: Four Stories From This Week

**1. Claude can now read your clients' bank accounts**

Anthropic is [testing a Personal Finance hub](https://www.engadget.com/2307936/now-claude-can-help-with-your-finances-too/) for US Pro, Max, and Ultra subscribers. It connects to more than 12,000 financial institutions through Plaid and can flag duplicate charges, surprise fees, and bills that have crept up, as well as build spending charts and budgets. It's read-only ("can't move, manage or invest your money"), covers up to two years of transactions, and Anthropic says conversations in the hub won't be used for training. Notably, Anthropic says it's exploring ways to work with licensed professionals and has invited banks and wealth managers to get in touch.

**My take:** If you're an advisor, assume some of your clients will walk into their next review having already had Claude go through their spending. That's good news. The budgeting conversation, which nobody enjoys, gets easier, and you get to spend the meeting on planning, taxes, and the things that need a human. Treat it as a reason to ask clients what they've learned, not a threat. And watch the "work with wealth managers" line closely. That's an invitation.

**2. Visa tested AI shopping agents. They failed 43% of the time.**

Visa's new [Agentic Commerce Benchmark](https://www.pymnts.com/news/artificial-intelligence/2026/visa-finds-ai-models-fail-43percent-shopping-test-runs/) ran 22 models from 12 labs through realistic shopping tasks, like buying a TV or a laptop. Overall, 57.2% of runs passed and 42.8% failed. The best models passed 82.2% of the time, and the worst only 28.9%. The worrying part: in 54% of failures, the agent **bought something anyway**, either an item that didn't meet the requirements or one chosen before it had asked enough questions.

**My take:** This is the clearest data I've seen on why I keep telling you to keep a human approving anything that moves money. An agent that confidently buys the wrong laptop is annoying. An agent that confidently executes the wrong transaction in a client account is a compliance event. Let agents research, compare, and draft. Keep humans on the button.

**3. Anthropic's IPO valuation is coming down to earth**

As Anthropic heads toward its Nasdaq listing, investors are reportedly discussing a valuation of [roughly $1.2–1.3 trillion](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/anthropic-ipo-valuation-discussions-fall-to-13-trillion-bloomberg-reports-5008125), according to Bloomberg. That's below the $1.4–1.5 trillion range given to bankers and well below the roughly $2 trillion figures floated in early October. Meanwhile, OpenAI [reportedly raised another $11 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/06/openai-reportedly-raises-an-additional-11-billion-at-a-1-25-trillion-valuation/).

**My take:** For anyone managing money, this will be one of the most-discussed listings of the decade, and clients will ask about it. For anyone *using* Claude, the valuation matters less than what comes with it: public-company pressure to show business revenue. Expect more enterprise features, more industry-specific tools (see story #1), and probably more attention to pricing. If Apple is rationing, the vendors are noticing.

**4. Voters said no to data centers in their backyards**

On Election Day, opponents of data centers won most of the local ballot measures, and dozens of measures across 10 states sought to restrict construction. NBC's exit poll found 76% of voters would oppose a data center in their own community. Separately, the International Data Center Authority counts at least [46 AI data center projects](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/06/ai-data-center-midterms-selloff.html), representing more than $170 billion, that were blocked, withdrawn, or stalled by local pushback between January 2024 and May 2026.

**My take:** This won't change what you can do with AI next quarter. Barclays makes the fair point that local opposition makes the buildout more expensive rather than stopping it. But scarce power means more expensive compute, and more expensive compute means more Apple-style rationing across the market. One more reason to measure what you're getting. Firms with a scorecard will absorb price increases easily. Firms without one will be the first to cut.
